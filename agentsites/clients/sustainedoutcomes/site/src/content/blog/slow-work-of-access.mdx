A season of fieldwork with regional land trusts left us with one stubborn finding. The "access to nature" programs that produce durable outcomes — the ones whose participation curves go up year-on-year and whose alumni keep showing up — share a property that most program design doesn't account for.

They move at the speed of trust.

## What that actually means in practice

Trust takes the form of small commitments kept over long periods. A weekly bird walk for two years. A field-research week each summer for five. A relationship with a public-school district that doesn't reset every time the principal changes.

The institutions best positioned to do this work are usually the ones least set up to wait. Land trusts run on grant cycles. School districts run on academic years. The communities the work serves run on lifetimes.

> Programs that try to compress that mismatch — by accelerating engagement, by scaling reach faster than relationship — tend to produce the participation curves you'd expect from a quarter-on-quarter business.

## A small operating principle

The principle we've started using when we scope this kind of work:

- **Year one is for showing up.** No participation targets. No reach metrics. The work is to demonstrate that the institution will keep its appointments.
- **Year two is for steady cadence.** A predictable monthly or weekly rhythm, however small. Numbers stay low; consistency is the metric.
- **Year three is when the curve turns.** Reach starts to grow, but from a foundation of relationships that have already survived a year of weather, transitions, and broken promises elsewhere.

If you start the curve at year one, you compress the foundation and the work tips over by year three. If you start at year three with the right foundation, the curve keeps going.

## What this asks of funders

Mostly, it asks for three-year general operating support that doesn't ratchet annual reach targets. It asks for the reporting cadence to match the work — quarterly check-ins, yes, but with the understanding that the meaningful evidence won't show up for eighteen months.

The funders we've seen do this well don't necessarily fund more. They fund longer, and they fund more quietly.

## A field note

The most common mistake — and we have made it ourselves — is to confuse the participation curve for the work. The work is the showing up. The curve is the trailing indicator.
